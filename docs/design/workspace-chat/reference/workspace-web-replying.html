2.1 Affordances and signifiers

In interaction design, an affordance describes a relationship between an object and an agent: what actions are possible for this agent with this object. A touchscreen region that responds to a swipe affords swiping, whether or not anyone can tell. A signifier is different. It is a perceivable cue, such as a label, an icon, a shadow or an arrow, that communicates where an action is possible and how to perform it. Designers often blur the two terms. We keep them apart because our three conditions hold the affordance constant (the schedule feature is equally possible in all of them) while varying only the signifier.

2.2 Feedback and the gulf of execution

The gulf of execution is the gap between what a person wants to do and what the system lets them do in a way they can see. Signifiers help bridge it before an action. Feedback works after the action: it tells the person what happened, so they can judge whether the goal was reached (bridging the gulf of evaluation). Prior lab work on mobile settings screens suggests that missing feedback leads to repeated actions and lower confidence, but those studies did not vary the signifier at the same time.

2.3 Hidden gestures

Gesture-only controls save screen space and look clean, which explains their popularity in minimalist home-automation apps. Their cost is that nothing on screen signals them.
